## Collecting more light

A bigger photodiode collects more light, but it also has more capacitance (slower) and more dark current. Optics offer a better deal: a lens or **concentrator** collects light over a large aperture and funnels it onto a small, fast detector.

There is a hard limit, set by conservation of **étendue** (or radiance): you cannot concentrate light from a wide range of angles into a small area. For an ideal non-imaging concentrator with refractive index $n$ and acceptance half-angle $\Psi_c$ <Cite id="kahn1997" />:

$$
g(\psi) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{n^2}{\sin^2\Psi_c}, & 0 \le \psi \le \Psi_c \\[0.6em] 0, & \psi > \Psi_c \end{cases}
$$

A hemispherical lens with $n=1.5$ and a 90° FOV gives $g = 2.25$; narrowing the FOV to 30° raises it to 9. The **compound parabolic concentrator** (CPC) approaches this ideal for one acceptance angle.

## Filtering out the sun

Background light reaches the detector across the whole spectrum, while the signal sits in a narrow band. A **bandpass optical filter** with transmission $T_s(\lambda)$ removes most ambient power (and hence shot noise) while passing the signal. Thin-film interference filters are very selective, but their passband shifts towards shorter wavelengths at oblique incidence, so wide FOVs need wider filters. The blue filter used with phosphor LEDs is a coarse example.

## The transimpedance amplifier

The photocurrent (microamps) must become a voltage. A **transimpedance amplifier** (TIA) does this with a feedback resistor $R_F$: $v_{\text{out}} = -R_F\, i_p$. Three quantities are locked together:

- **Gain** $R_F$: bigger is better for small signals.
- **Noise**: the resistor contributes $\sqrt{4kT/R_F}$ A/√Hz, so a bigger $R_F$ is *quieter*.
- **Bandwidth**: with an op-amp of gain-bandwidth product GBW and total input capacitance $C_T$ (photodiode plus amplifier),
$$
f_{3\text{dB}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\text{GBW}}{2\pi R_F C_T}}.
$$
A bigger $R_F$ or a bigger photodiode is *slower*.

<Callout type="key">
The receiver designer's triangle is **area, bandwidth and noise**. Large detectors collect more light but are slow and noisy; optics help collect light without adding capacitance; and $R_F$ trades gain and noise against speed. High-speed LiFi receivers usually pair a small photodiode with a concentrator and a low-capacitance TIA.
</Callout>
