## Light has more than brightness

An IM/DD link only uses the *power* of the light. But a beam also has a **direction**, a **wavelength** and a **polarization**, the direction in which its electric field oscillates. Passive optical components act on these, and many optical wireless tricks come from them: separating colours for WDM, rejecting glare, isolating a laser from its own reflections, and encoding qubits for quantum key distribution.

A general (possibly partly polarized) beam is described by the **Stokes vector** $S = (S_0, S_1, S_2, S_3)$: total power, horizontal-vs-vertical, $+45°$-vs-$-45°$, and right-vs-left circular. Each component is a $4\times4$ **Mueller matrix** $M$, and the output is simply $S_{out} = M\,S_{in}$. The figure below does exactly that.

## Polarizers

A linear polarizer passes the field component along its axis. For polarized light at angle $\theta$ to that axis, the transmitted power follows **Malus's law**

$$
P_{out} = P_{in}\cos^2\theta.
$$

An unpolarized LED loses half its power in the first polarizer. Wire-grid polarizers (fine metal lines, also used in LCD panels), absorbing films and crystal prisms (Glan–Taylor) do the same job with different extinction ratios, power limits and acceptance angles.

## Wave plates

A wave plate is a birefringent crystal: light polarized along its *fast axis* travels faster than along its *slow axis*, so one component is delayed by a retardance $\delta$.

<Card title="Half-wave plate, δ = π">Rotates linear polarization: with its fast axis at θ it turns linear light at angle α into linear light at 2θ − α. Used to align a laser's polarization to a PBS or modulator.</Card>

## Beam splitters

| Type | What it splits by | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Plate or cube, non-polarizing (NPBS) | power, e.g. 50:50 or 90:10 | monitoring, interferometers, multi-receiver taps |
| **Polarizing (PBS)** | polarization: H transmitted, V reflected | polarization multiplexing, QKD receivers, isolators |
| Dichroic mirror | wavelength | WDM, separating an infrared uplink from a visible downlink |
| Fibre coupler or photonic lantern | modes and power | coupling FSO light into fibre |

A beam splitter divides *power*; it never creates any. A 50:50 splitter feeding two detectors halves the signal at each, which is why splitting is used for diversity or monitoring, not for free gain.

## Isolators and circulators

A **Faraday rotator** turns polarization by 45° in the *same absolute sense* whichever way the light travels (the effect is non-reciprocal, set by a magnetic field). Put it between polarizers at 0° and 45° and forward light passes; light reflected back is rotated a further 45°, arrives at 90° to the first polarizer and is blocked. That is an **optical isolator**, and narrow-linewidth lasers in FSO terminals need one, because back-reflections destabilise them. Try the *Isolator* preset.

## Where these show up in optical wireless

- **Glare and ambient rejection.** Sunlight reflected from water or glass is strongly polarized; a polarizer in front of the receiver removes much of it.
- **Polarization multiplexing.** Two independent data streams on H and V double a laser link's capacity; a PBS separates them. With LEDs, liquid-crystal polarization modulation drives systems such as POLI <Cite id="chan2017poli" />.
- **Colour separation.** Dichroic splitters and filters separate the channels of RGB-LED WDM links <Cite id="chun2016wdm" />.
- **Quantum key distribution.** A BB84 receiver is a 50:50 splitter followed by two PBSs, one preceded by a half-wave plate at 22.5° so it measures the 45° basis <Cite id="bennett1984" />. See the <LessonLink id="quantum-owc">quantum lesson</LessonLink>.
