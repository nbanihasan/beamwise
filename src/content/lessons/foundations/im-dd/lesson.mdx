## Two ways to put data on a wave

A radio transmitter controls the **electric field** of its carrier: amplitude *and* phase, which is why radio can use complex constellations like QAM directly. At the receiver, an antenna and a mixer recover that field.

Light is also an electromagnetic wave, but its carrier oscillates at around 500 THz. No practical electronics can follow that field. Practical optical wireless systems therefore use a far simpler scheme, **intensity modulation with direct detection (IM/DD)**:

- the transmitter varies the **instantaneous optical power** $x(t)$ of an LED or laser;
- the receiver's photodiode produces a current proportional to the **incident power**, ignoring phase entirely.

<KeyIdea>
In IM/DD the information signal is the optical power itself. Power cannot be negative and has no phase, so the transmitted signal must be **real** and **non-negative**: $x(t) \ge 0$.
</KeyIdea>

## The IM/DD channel model

Kahn and Barry wrote the baseband model that the whole field uses <Cite id="kahn1997" />:

$$
y(t) = R\, x(t) \otimes h(t) + n(t)
$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $x(t)$ | instantaneous transmitted optical power (W) |
| $h(t)$ | channel impulse response; its DC gain is $H(0) = \int h(t)\,dt$ |
| $R$ | photodiode responsivity (A/W) |
| $y(t)$ | photocurrent (A) |
| $n(t)$ | noise current, usually modelled as signal-independent Gaussian |

It looks like an ordinary linear channel, but with two unusual constraints on the input:

$$
x(t) \ge 0, \qquad \lim_{T\to\infty} \frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} x(t)\,dt = P_{\text{avg}} \le P_{\max}.
$$

The second constraint, on **average** optical power, comes from eye safety and illumination requirements. In radio, the constraint is on average *electrical* power, $\overline{x^2}$. This changes which modulation formats are efficient: formats with a low duty cycle, such as PPM, become attractive because they concentrate energy into short pulses.

## Bias and clipping

Most information signals, such as a sine wave or an OFDM waveform, are bipolar. To transmit them we add a DC bias $b$ and let the LED clip anything that would go below zero:

$$
x(t) = \max\big(0,\; b + s(t)\big).
$$

The bias costs power that carries no information. In visible light communication it is free in a sense, because the room needs to be lit anyway, and the bias is the illumination. In infrared links and battery-powered transmitters, it is pure overhead. This tension drives the optical OFDM variants in <LessonLink id="optical-ofdm" />. Armstrong's tutorial develops this in detail <Cite id="armstrong2009" />.

## Electrical vs optical SNR

Because the receiver converts power to current, the **electrical** signal power scales with the *square* of the optical power:

$$
\text{SNR} = \frac{(R\,P_r)^2}{\sigma_n^2}.
$$

<MathBox>
Double the received optical power and the electrical SNR goes up by a factor of four (6 dB), not two. Path loss in optical wireless is therefore twice as painful in electrical dB: a 3 dB optical loss is a 6 dB SNR loss. Textbooks distinguish carefully between the two scales <Cite id="ghassemlooy2019book" />.
</MathBox>

## Why not coherent detection?

Coherent optical receivers exist and dominate long-haul fibre. They mix the incoming light with a local laser to recover the field. In free space this is hard:

- LEDs are **incoherent**: their spectrum is tens of nanometres wide, so there is no stable phase to track.
- Coherent detection needs the local oscillator and the signal to overlap in a single spatial mode. Wide-field receivers for mobile users collect light from many spatial modes at once.
- IM/DD hardware is cheap: an LED, a photodiode and an amplifier.

Some laser-based FSO and satellite links do use coherent detection. For indoor VLC and LiFi, IM/DD is effectively universal <Cite id="karunatilaka2015" />.

## A receiver averages over a huge area

A photodiode is thousands to millions of wavelengths across. This has a welcome side effect. Multipath fading, which plagues radio, mostly averages out: the speckle pattern of optical interference is far smaller than the detector, so the detector sees the sum of powers, not a fluctuating sum of fields. Indoor optical channels therefore show **no small-scale fading**, only slowly varying path loss and dispersion. That is why the channel lessons focus on geometry and delay spread, not Rayleigh statistics.
