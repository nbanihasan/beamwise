## Where the noise comes from

A photodiode's output contains the signal and three main kinds of noise <Cite id="komine2004" /> <Cite id="kahn1997" />:

<Steps>
<li><div><strong>Signal shot noise.</strong> Photons and electrons arrive at random times (Poisson statistics), so even a perfectly steady light produces a fluctuating current.</div></li>
<li><div><strong>Background shot noise.</strong> Sunlight, lamps and other luminaires add a large DC photocurrent $I_{\text{bg}}$. Its DC value is easily removed, but its shot noise is not. Indoors near a window this often dominates.</div></li>
<li><div><strong>Thermal noise.</strong> Johnson noise of the load or feedback resistor and the amplifier's own input noise. It does not depend on the light at all.</div></li>
</Steps>

All three are, to a very good approximation, zero-mean, Gaussian and independent, so their **variances add**:

$$
\sigma^2 = \underbrace{2qR P_r B}_{\text{signal shot}} + \underbrace{2q I_{\text{bg}} B}_{\text{background shot}} + \underbrace{\frac{4kT}{R_F} B}_{\text{thermal}}
$$

with $q$ the electron charge, $B$ the noise bandwidth, $k$ Boltzmann's constant, $T$ temperature and $R_F$ the transimpedance feedback resistance. Datasheets often quote amplifier noise directly as a current density $i_n$ in pA/√Hz, giving a thermal term $i_n^2 B$.

<Callout type="math" title="Komine's thermal-noise expression">
Komine and Nakagawa use a fuller model of a FET preamplifier, with feedback-resistor noise and channel noise that grows with photodiode capacitance:
$$
\sigma^2_{\text{th}} = \frac{8\pi k T_K}{G}\eta A I_2 B^2 + \frac{16\pi^2 k T_K \Gamma}{g_m}\eta^2 A^2 I_3 B^3
$$
Larger detectors (larger $\eta A$, capacitance per area times area) are noisier at high bandwidth. This is one form of the area–bandwidth trade-off explored in <LessonLink id="optics-frontend" />.
</Callout>

## SNR and bit error rate

With electrical SNR defined as $\text{SNR} = (R P_r)^2/\sigma^2$, on-off keying with a midpoint threshold has

$$
\text{BER}_{\text{OOK}} = Q\big(\sqrt{\text{SNR}}\big), \qquad Q(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_x^\infty e^{-u^2/2}du.
$$

A BER of $10^{-6}$ needs $\text{SNR} \approx 13.5$ dB; $10^{-9}$ needs about 15.6 dB.

## Two regimes

Look at the slope of SNR against received optical power in the figure:

- **Thermal-limited** (weak signals): $\sigma^2$ is fixed, so $\text{SNR} \propto P_r^2$: **2 dB per dB**.
- **Shot-limited** (strong signals or strong ambient light): $\sigma^2 \propto P_r$ or $I_{\text{bg}}$, so SNR rises only **1 dB per dB**, or not at all if background dominates.

Indoor VLC receivers are usually thermal-limited in the dark and background-limited in daylight. That is why optical filters, narrow FOVs and AC coupling are so valuable: they reduce $I_{\text{bg}}$.

## Avalanche gain and its optimum

An avalanche photodiode multiplies the photocurrent by $M$ before the amplifier. The signal power grows as $M^2$ and so does shot noise, but shot noise is also multiplied by the **excess noise factor** $F(M) = k_A M + (1-k_A)(2-1/M)$:

$$
\text{SNR}_{\text{APD}} = \frac{(M R P_r)^2}{2q(RP_r + I_{\text{bg}})M^2F(M)B + i_n^2 B}.
$$

At low gain the amplifier noise is beaten down; at high gain the excess noise wins. The optimum $M$ is where they balance, typically 10–100 for silicon. Background light pushes the optimum lower. Detector physics is covered in <LessonLink id="photodetectors" />.

## A link budget in five lines

| Step | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Transmit power | 2 W optical → +33 dBm |
| Channel gain $H(0)$ | $4\times10^{-6}$ → −54 dB |
| Received power | −21 dBm (8 µW) |
| Photocurrent | 0.5 A/W × 8 µW = 4 µA |
| Noise (20 MHz, 5 pA/√Hz, 50 µA ambient) | σ ≈ 29 nA → SNR ≈ 43 dB |

43 dB is far more than OOK needs, which is why VLC links can afford spectrally efficient formats such as 64-QAM OFDM. Pathak *et al.* summarise typical noise conditions in practical deployments <Cite id="pathak2015survey" />.
