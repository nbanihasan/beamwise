## Seven ways to lose a photon

Between the transmitter and the receiver, light can be destroyed, redirected, defocused, steered away or simply spread too thin. Each of these has a different signature in the received signal, so each needs a different fix. ## Absorption: photons destroyed

Molecules absorb photons at wavelengths matching their energy levels, and the energy becomes heat. Over a path of length $L$ with absorption coefficient $a$:

$$
P(L) = P_0\, e^{-aL}.
$$

The coefficient depends strongly on wavelength. Water absorbs red and infrared very strongly and blue-green weakly, which is why underwater links use 450–532 nm <Cite id="zeng2017uwoc" />. The atmosphere has transmission windows around 850 nm and 1550 nm, which is why FSO uses those bands <Cite id="khalighi2014fso" />.

## Scattering: photons redirected

Particles change a photon's direction without destroying it. How much and where depends on particle size $r$ relative to wavelength $\lambda$:

<Compare>
<Card title="Rayleigh (r ≪ λ)">Air molecules. Strength grows as λ⁻⁴: blue scatters far more than red (that is why the sky is blue). Roughly symmetric forward/backward.</Card>
<Card title="Mie (r ≈ λ)">Fog, haze, plankton, silt. Strongly forward-peaked. The dominant loss in fog and in coastal water.</Card>
<Card title="Geometric (r ≫ λ)">Rain drops, snow. Roughly wavelength-independent, so moving to 1550 nm does not help in rain.</Card>
</Compare>

Total extinction is $c = a + b$ (absorption plus scattering). The **phase function** describes where scattered light goes. Simulators usually use the Henyey–Greenstein form with asymmetry $g = \langle\cos\theta\rangle$: $g \approx 0.92$ is typical for ocean water, so most scattering is a small deflection <Cite id="kaushal2016uwoc" />. Scattered light is not lost for good. It arrives later (time dispersion, so ISI) and from wider angles, so a wide field-of-view receiver can collect it. Ultraviolet links even rely on atmospheric scattering to communicate around obstacles <Cite id="xu2008uv" />.

<KeyIdea title="Absorption vs scattering in one sentence">
Absorption lowers the power. Scattering lowers the power *in the beam* but spreads it in angle and time, so a receiver with a wide FOV can collect part of it back, at the cost of delay spread.
</KeyIdea>

## Turbulence: beam wander and scintillation

Temperature differences make the refractive index of air (or water) fluctuate in small, moving cells, each a weak lens. Its strength is the structure constant $C_n^2$; its accumulated effect over a path is the **Rytov variance** <Cite id="andrews2005book" />:

$$
\sigma_R^2 = 1.23\, C_n^2\, k^{7/6} L^{11/6}.
$$

Two effects matter for communications:

- **Beam wander.** Eddies larger than the beam steer it as a whole. The spot dances around the receiver, as in **Turbulence** above. Worst for uplinks, where turbulence sits next to the transmitter <Cite id="kaushal2017space" />.
- **Scintillation.** Eddies smaller than the beam make parts of the wavefront interfere, so the spot breaks into speckle and the received power fades by many dB in milliseconds, as in **Scintillation**. The gamma-gamma distribution describes the irradiance from weak to strong turbulence <Cite id="alhabash2001" />.

Fades last about a millisecond, which is millions of bits at Gb/s, so plain FEC cannot ride through them. Mitigations are **aperture averaging** (a receiver larger than the speckle averages it out), **spatial diversity** (several apertures fading independently), **adaptive optics**, and long **interleavers** with strong codes.

## Geometry: divergence and pointing

Every beam spreads with full divergence $\theta$. For a receiver aperture $D$ at range $L$, the captured fraction is roughly

$$
h_g \approx \left(\frac{D}{w_0 + \theta L}\right)^2 \quad (\le 1).
$$

A 1 mrad beam is already 1 m wide after 1 km, and a 5 cm aperture then catches only about 0.25 % of the light. Narrowing the beam helps, but then **pointing error** (building sway, vibration, a hovering drone) moves the spot off the receiver. Real systems balance the two: narrow beams with active tracking for long links (satellites use µrad beams with fast steering mirrors), wider beams with no tracking for short ones.

## Weather

Fog is the worst case: droplets about the size of the wavelength sit in the Mie regime and attenuate by tens to hundreds of dB/km. The Kim model links attenuation to visibility $V$ <Cite id="kim2001" />. Rain costs a few to ~20 dB/km, snow more. Because fog barely affects radio and rain barely affects light at short range, **hybrid RF/FSO** links back each other up.

## Summary

| Impairment | What happens to photons | Typical signature | Main mitigations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absorption | destroyed | exponential power loss | choose the wavelength window, more power |
| Scattering | redirected | power loss + delay spread | wide FOV, equalisation, or use it (UV NLOS) |
| Beam wander | whole beam steered | slow fades | wider beam, tracking |
| Scintillation | speckle | fast deep fades | aperture averaging, diversity, interleaved FEC |
| Divergence | spread out | range-squared loss | collimate, larger receive aperture |
| Pointing error | miss the aperture | random outages | tracking, wider beam |
| Fog, rain, snow | Mie / geometric scattering | weather outages | hybrid RF/FSO, site diversity |
