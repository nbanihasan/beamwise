## Two questions classify every link

Kahn and Barry's classic tutorial sorts indoor optical links with two questions <Cite id="kahn1997" />:

1. **Is there a line of sight (LOS)** between transmitter and receiver, or does all the light arrive by reflection (NLOS)?
2. **How directional are the ends?** A *directed* link has a narrow beam and a narrow field of view; a *non-directed* one has wide ones; a *hybrid* mixes them.

That gives a small family of designs. Switch between them below, then walk the person through the room.

<LinkGeometries />

## The four you meet most

<Compare>
<Card title="Directed LOS">Narrow beam meets narrow FOV. Highest SNR, smallest delay spread, rejects ambient light, so Gb/s are easy. One hand in the way and the link is gone. Used in FSO and point-to-point infrared.</Card>
<Card title="Non-directed LOS">Wide beam from the ceiling, wide-FOV receiver. No aiming, reflections soften shadows. Moderate path loss and delay spread. The LiFi downlink.</Card>
<Card title="Diffuse (NLOS)">The transmitter illuminates the ceiling, which re-radiates like a huge Lambertian source <Cite id="gfeller1979" />. Survives almost any blockage and orientation, but costs 10–20 dB more path loss and has delay spreads of several ns: rates of tens of Mb/s, not Gb/s.</Card>
<Card title="Tracked">A narrow beam steered toward a moving user. Directed-LOS performance with mobility, at the cost of beam-steering hardware (MEMS mirrors, liquid-crystal or LED-array steering).</Card>
</Compare>

## What a blocker does

When the person steps into the line of sight, the large first spike in the impulse response vanishes. What remains is the diffuse tail: weaker and spread in time. In a non-directed link that typically costs 10–20 dB, often enough to drop the modulation order but not to lose the link entirely; in a directed link the link is simply gone.

$$
H_{\text{blocked}}(0) = H_{\text{diff}}(0) \ll H_{\text{LOS}}(0) + H_{\text{diff}}(0)
$$

<KeyIdea title="The fundamental trade-off">
Directionality buys SNR and bandwidth and costs robustness. Every practical system picks a point on that curve, then adds tricks to recover what it gave up.
</KeyIdea>

## Recovering from blockage

- **Overlapping cells.** Several luminaires cover each spot, so blocking one leaves another (LiFi handover, <LessonLink id="lifi-networks" />) <Cite id="haas2016lifi" />.
- **Angle diversity.** Several narrow-FOV detectors pointing in different directions; select or combine the best (see <LessonLink id="lens-systems" />).
- **Reconfigurable intelligent surfaces and mirrors.** A wall surface that redirects light around an obstacle creates a controllable NLOS path <Cite id="aboagye2023ris" />. You can try this with a mirror in the <a href="#/bench?scenario=corridor">Optical Bench</a>.
- **Hybrid RF/optical.** Fall back to Wi-Fi when light is blocked.
