## Walls are mirrors, roughly

Painted walls, ceilings and furniture reflect 30–90 % of visible light. Most indoor surfaces are close to **diffuse (Lambertian) reflectors**: whatever direction light arrives from, they re-emit it in a $\cos\theta$ pattern. Every wall element therefore behaves like a weak secondary LED with $m = 1$.

Light that bounces travels further than the direct ray, so it arrives later. At $c \approx 0.3$ m/ns, an extra 3 m of path is 10 ns of delay. At 100 Mb/s, a symbol lasts 10 ns.

## First-order reflections

Split a wall into small elements of area $dA_{\text{wall}}$ with reflectivity $\rho$. The element first acts as a receiver of the LED and then as a Lambertian transmitter towards the detector. Its contribution to the channel gain is <Cite id="kahn1997" />:

$$
dH_{\text{ref}}(0) = \frac{(m+1)A\,\rho\, dA_{\text{wall}}}{2\pi^2 d_1^2 d_2^2}\cos^m(\phi)\cos(\alpha)\cos(\beta)\,T_s(\psi)\,g(\psi)\cos(\psi),
$$

arriving with delay $(d_1 + d_2)/c$. Here $d_1$ is the LED–wall distance, $d_2$ the wall–detector distance, $\alpha$ the incidence angle at the wall and $\beta$ the re-emission angle. Higher-order bounces follow the same recursion. Barry *et al.* introduced the recursive method that the field still uses <Cite id="barry1993" />.

## Delay spread

From the impulse response $h(t)$, define the mean delay and the **RMS delay spread**:

$$
\bar\tau = \frac{\int t\, h(t)\,dt}{\int h(t)\,dt}, \qquad
\tau_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{\int (t-\bar\tau)^2 h(t)\,dt}{\int h(t)\,dt}}.
$$

$\tau_{\text{rms}}$ summarises how smeared the channel is. Two rules of thumb:

- the channel's coherence bandwidth is roughly $1/(5\tau_{\text{rms}})$;
- inter-symbol interference (ISI) stays small for symbol periods above about $10\,\tau_{\text{rms}}$, so single-carrier rates up to about $1/(10\tau_{\text{rms}})$ need no equaliser.

Typical indoor visible light channels have $\tau_{\text{rms}}$ between 1 and 10 ns when LOS dominates, and more in corners or with shadowing <Cite id="lee2011multipath" />.

<Callout type="key" title="LOS dominates, until it doesn't">
Under a luminaire, the LOS component is often 10–20 dB stronger than the diffuse tail, so the channel is nearly flat over tens of MHz. In practice the **LED's bandwidth** usually limits the rate long before multipath does. Near walls, in corners, or when the LOS path is blocked, the diffuse part takes over and dispersion matters. OFDM and equalisers (<LessonLink id="optical-ofdm" />, <LessonLink id="equalization" />) handle both cases.
</Callout>

## Link configurations

Kahn and Barry classify links by two choices: is the transmitter and receiver **directed** or not, and is there a **line of sight**?

## From the impulse response to a model

A useful closed-form approximation for the diffuse part is an exponential decay (the model used in the Link Lab):

$$
h(t) = H(0)\Big[(1-\kappa)\,\delta(t) + \frac{\kappa}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}\,u(t)\Big],
$$

where $\kappa$ is the share of power arriving by reflection and $\tau$ sets the tail length. Its frequency response is a flat floor $(1-\kappa)$ plus a low-pass term with corner at $1/(2\pi\tau)$. For simulation recipes, see Chapter 3 of Ghassemlooy *et al.* <Cite id="ghassemlooy2019book" />.
