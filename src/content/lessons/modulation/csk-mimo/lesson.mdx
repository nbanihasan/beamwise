## Colour as a dimension

An RGB luminaire has three independently drivable emitters. Two very different ways to use them:

- **Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM)**: send *independent* streams on each colour and separate them with colour filters at the receiver. Four off-the-shelf RGBY LEDs with bit-loaded OFDM reached 15.73 Gb/s <Cite id="bian2019" />.
- **Colour-shift keying (CSK)**: send *one* stream whose symbols are **colours**.

### CSK

CSK was standardised as PHY III of IEEE 802.15.7 <Cite id="ieee802157" />. The three LED colours define a triangle on the CIE 1931 $(x, y)$ chromaticity diagram. Any colour inside it can be produced by mixing the three with weights $P_i/P$:

$$
x_s = \sum_i \frac{P_i}{P} x_i, \qquad y_s = \sum_i \frac{P_i}{P} y_i, \qquad \sum_i P_i = P.
$$

Symbols are points inside the triangle: 4, 8 or 16 of them.

<CskDiagram />

<KeyIdea>
CSK keeps the **total optical power constant**. Only the colour ratio changes, and fast enough that the eye sees the time-averaged colour, the centroid of the constellation. That makes CSK inherently flicker-free and keeps illumination unaffected, which is its main advantage over intensity schemes.
</KeyIdea>

The receiver uses three colour-filtered photodiodes, inverts the colour mixing (including crosstalk between filters) and decides on the nearest constellation point. Wider LED spacing in wavelength gives a bigger triangle and more distance between symbols.

## Space as a dimension: optical MIMO

Ceilings have many LEDs, and receivers can have several detectors. In radio, multipath makes MIMO channels rich and well-conditioned. In optics there is no phase and little multipath, so the channel gains from nearby LEDs to nearby detectors are **almost identical** <Cite id="zeng2009mimo" />:

$$
\mathbf{y} = R\,\mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{n}, \qquad H_{ij} = H_{\text{LOS}}(\text{LED}_j \to \text{PD}_i).
$$

When the rows of $\mathbf H$ are nearly equal, the matrix is close to rank one. Its **condition number** explodes, and inverting it (zero-forcing) amplifies noise enormously.

<MimoLab />

Fixes:

- **Spread the detectors** or tilt them in different directions (angle diversity).
- Use an **imaging receiver**: a lens maps each LED onto different pixels, making $\mathbf H$ nearly diagonal <Cite id="zeng2009mimo" />.
- Block some paths with apertures or partitions.

## Three MIMO techniques

Fath and Haas compared the main techniques for indoor OWC <Cite id="fath2013mimo" />:

<Compare>
  <Card title="Repetition coding (RC)">All LEDs send the same symbol. Pure power and diversity gain, no multiplexing, robust everywhere. Often the best choice when H is poorly conditioned.</Card>
  <Card title="Spatial multiplexing (SMP)">Each LED sends an independent stream; the receiver inverts H. Highest rate when H is well conditioned (imaging receivers).</Card>
  <Card title="Spatial modulation (SM)">Only one LED is on at a time; its *index* carries log₂(Nₜ) extra bits <Cite id="mesleh2011sm" />. No inter-channel interference, but needs distinct channel gains between LEDs.</Card>
</Compare>
