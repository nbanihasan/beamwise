## Why OFDM

An LED's low-pass response and multipath dispersion both make the channel **frequency selective**: some frequencies arrive strongly, others weakly. OFDM splits a wide channel into $N$ narrow **subcarriers**, each so narrow that the channel is flat across it. Three consequences make it the modulation behind almost every multi-Gb/s VLC record <Cite id="tsonev2014" /> <Cite id="islim2017" />:

1. Equalisation becomes one complex multiplication per subcarrier ("one-tap").
2. A cyclic prefix longer than the delay spread removes inter-symbol interference.
3. Each subcarrier can carry a different constellation, matched to its SNR (**bit loading**). Good subcarriers carry 64-QAM, weak ones 4-QAM or nothing. In wired systems this is called discrete multitone (DMT).

## The problem: OFDM is complex and bipolar

An OFDM transmitter takes QAM symbols $X[k]$ and computes an inverse FFT:

$$
x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\, e^{j2\pi kn/N}.
$$

In radio, the real and imaginary parts drive the I and Q branches of a mixer. An LED has no I and Q, and it cannot emit negative light (<LessonLink id="im-dd" />). The output must be **real** and then made **non-negative**.

## Step 1: Hermitian symmetry gives a real signal

If the input satisfies

$$
X[N-k] = X^*[k], \qquad X[0] = X[N/2] = 0,
$$

then every pair of mirrored terms in the IFFT adds to a real cosine, and $x[n]$ is real. The price: only $N/2 - 1$ of the $N$ subcarriers carry independent data. Optical OFDM has half the spectral efficiency of complex baseband OFDM over the same bandwidth <Cite id="armstrong2009" />.

## Step 2: make it non-negative

<MathBox title="Why ACO clipping is harmless on odd subcarriers">
Write the clipped signal as $x_c[n] = \tfrac12\big(x[n] + |x[n]|\big)$. With only odd subcarriers loaded, $x[n + N/2] = -x[n]$ (anti-symmetry), so $|x[n]|$ is periodic with period $N/2$. Its spectrum therefore lives only on even subcarriers. The odd subcarriers of $x_c$ are exactly $\tfrac12 X[k]$.
</MathBox>

## PAPR and clipping

The sum of many independent subcarriers is nearly Gaussian, so its **peak-to-average power ratio** (PAPR) is high, often 10–13 dB. An LED has a limited linear range: peaks get clipped, adding **clipping noise** that spreads across all subcarriers and sets an error floor. Designers choose the bias and the scaling to balance clipping at the bottom (DCO) and the top (saturation) against the SNR. This is one of the central trade-offs in optical OFDM <Cite id="armstrong2009" />.
